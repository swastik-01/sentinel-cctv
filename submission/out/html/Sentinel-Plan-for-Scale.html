Sentinel's statewide design rests on three decisions, each forced by a measurement or a property of the camera estate rather than by preference:

  1. Not every camera needs continuous analytics. Cameras are tiered by what their position allows, and the registry records it (anpr_viable, analytics_enabled).
  2. Video stays where it is. Departments keep recording on their own NVR/VMS. Analytics runs at district edge nodes; only metadata crosses the backbone.
  3. Frames never touch CPU memory at scale. Our own benchmark shows the CPU, not the GPU, is what runs out first — so production nodes decode and infer on the GPU.

Every sizing and cost number below is generated by bench/capacity.py (python bench/capacity.py; --help lists every input). Each input is labelled GIVEN (the problem statement or the PoC's own configuration), MEASURED (on the PoC, dated) or ASSUMPTION, and all of them are listed in Appendix A. Change an assumption and re-run. Unit prices are placeholders to be replaced by GeM quotes. Anything marked design is specified here but not built in the PoC.

1. What we measured

sentinel-bench on the development laptop: GTX 1650 Ti (4 GB), Intel i7-10750H with 6 logical CPUs, 16 GB RAM, Windows 11 + Docker Desktop. Full pipeline per stream: software H.264/H.265 decode → YOLOv11n plate detector (CUDA, FP32) + text-line finder → batched OCR (fast-plate-ocr CCT-XS v2, CPU). Target: 5 analysed frames/s per stream.

1 (1080p25) 28 4.8 91 ms 26% 171 MB 96%
2 (1080p25) 56 4.8 130 ms 33% 171 MB 98%
4 (mixed) 89 3.5 222 ms 26% 253 MB 100%
8 (mixed) 160 1.3 452 ms 21% 493 MB 100%

Source: bench/results/bench_20260917_174834.json.

What this tells us

  • The GPU is idle; the CPU is saturated. GPU utilisation never exceeds a third. Software decode of every frame (inter-frame codecs cannot skip reference frames), colour conversion and image preprocessing consume the host.
  • Confounder, stated plainly: an antivirus service was using ~41% of the machine at idle during these runs. The absolute numbers are pessimistic; the shape — CPU-bound, GPU-idle — is the finding.
  • Optimisations already in the worker: grab-every-frame but convert only ~1.5× the analysis rate; batched OCR with capped threads; on-demand browser transcodes (measured 17 Sep: idle media-server CPU from ~130% to ~23% of a core); FP16 auto-disabled on GTX 16xx cards, which return NaN boxes in half precision.
  • Hardware decode, measured afterwards on the same laptop (24 Sep, bench/results/decode.md, two rounds, plate pipeline without object detection): with D3D11 decode through OpenCV (HW_DECODE=auto, now the worker's default) analysed fps per stream rose from 1.93 to 2.88 at 8 streams and from 0.91 to 1.34 at 12 (+49% both), and system CPU at 8 streams fell from 94% to 80%; at 4 streams nothing changed. On this laptop the D3D11 decoder is the Intel UHD integrated GPU's, not NVDEC (Windows GPU-engine counters, 24 Sep; the NVIDIA decoder stayed at 0%). It shows that decode offload relieves the CPU. NVDEC on the node is still unmeasured; on another laptop's GTX 1650, decode only (9 Oct, bench/results/nvdec_decode.md), the NVIDIA decoder took about 740 frames a second of 1080p H.264 at its ceiling (about 29 streams at 25 fps) and about 1,500 of 1080p H.265 with the resize on the GPU, and at 16 real-time 1080p streams cut system CPU from 89% to 31% (H.264, decoded and resized to 640 px) and from 75% to 32% (H.265). In today's worker frames still go to the CPU after decode, and a laptop result does not replace the per-node assumption in §6.2.

Consequence for production: GPU-native nodes. Hardware decode (NVDEC via GStreamer nvcodec or FFmpeg cuvid) keeps frames in GPU memory; preprocessing and inference run on the same device (ONNX Runtime CUDA/TensorRT execution provider); only boxes and crops return to the CPU. The worker's interfaces (RtspSource, PlateDetector, PlateOCR) are the seams where that swap happens without touching the rest of the platform.

Store-and-forward: a fully loaded node spools ~181 MB a day of sightings and counts; the PoC default cap (SPOOL_MAX_MB=512) covers 3.0 days, 1.3 GB covers 7. Production sets SPOOL_MAX_MB for the 7-day target. Since 8 Oct each worker process spools under its own folder (data/spool/<worker id>, with its watchlist copy and local-alerts file), so a plate worker and a counts-only worker on one node never replay each other's files; the cap is per worker.

Candidate nodes are benchmarked in the pilot with sentinel-bench. If another accelerator, or a two-GPU node, wins on cost per stream, the node count and the node price change together. In the PoC, decoded frames are handed to the CPU for preprocessing (§1); keeping them on the GPU from decode to inference is design.

The cost model carries no software licence fees (§9.3). Two licences carry obligations:

  • Ultralytics YOLO (AGPL-3.0). A government deployment publishes its changes under a compatible licence, or buys a commercial Ultralytics licence (not priced here), or retrains both detectors under a permissive framework. Both detectors run on Ultralytics YOLO, licensed AGPL-3.0. A licence-clean replacement is planned for the pilot, not the finale: YOLOX detectors (Apache-2.0) run by ONNX Runtime (MIT), with the plate model retrained only on images whose licences allow commercial use. Nothing has been trained. It replaces the current models only if it passes adoption gates we wrote down before training (bench/detector_gates.md): no loss of plate capture or exact reads on our own feeds, no loss of plate yield on the sandbox cameras, and no slower analysis. Until then the AGPL models are what runs, at the finale too, and every number in this pack was measured with them. We never quote the plate model's published scores: its own model card says they are inflated by an overlap between its training and test images. Every accuracy figure here is our own measurement. The plan covers the detectors only; the OCR model's training data is not documented by its author. Grafana and Loki are also AGPL-3.0, but they run unmodified as separate services, so their licence does not reach Sentinel's code.
  • react-leaflet (Hippocratic License 2.1), the map bindings in the PoC's front end. It is not an OSI-approved open-source licence: it requires use consistent with the UN human-rights principles and settles disputes by public arbitration. A police deployment has it cleared by legal review, or replaces it with direct calls to Leaflet (BSD-2).
  • PyAV (BSD-3) with a bundled GPL FFmpeg. The counts-only worker (7 Oct) decodes through the PyAV wheel, whose FFmpeg is a GPL build, used for decoding only. A deployment builds PyAV against its own LGPL FFmpeg, or accepts the GPL terms as it already does for the FFmpeg in the relay image; on the edge node the GPU decode of row 2 replaces it in any case.

4. Network & Bandwidth Planning

4.1 Camera → edge

Leg Cameras Per camera Average district Statewide
Tier A: main stream, continuous 8,000 4 Mbps 970 Mbps 32.0 Gbps
Tier B: sub-stream, continuous 16,000 0.8 Mbps 388 Mbps 12.8 Gbps
Tier C: only while watched 56,000 0 when idle ≤ 17.9 Mbps (one 16-tile wall) ≤ 609 Mbps (every wall)
Continuous total 24,000 1.36 Gbps 44.8 Gbps

Video stays inside the district network for analytics, but this leg is not small: the analysed cameras put about 1.36 Gbps into an average district's edge. Averages hide the skew, since cameras concentrate in the cities, so each district's network is sized from its registry count during onboarding. Three consequences:

  • The edge rack needs 10 GbE aggregation from the camera VLANs, priced as district network works in §9.2 and confirmed by the site survey.
  • A camera behind a thin link (a remote checkpost on a cellular link, say) cannot be analysed at the district edge at 4 Mbps. For those sites the design option is a small edge box beside the camera or NVR, not priced here.
  • Tier B pulls the camera's sub-stream. The object detector already resizes every frame to 640 px (object_imgsz), so a 640p stream gives it about the same pixels at a fifth of the bitrate. Tier A needs the main stream: the plate is read from the full-resolution crop.

4.2 Edge → core and viewing

Flow Rate Note
Edge → core: metadata 2.7 Mbps average; 556 sightings/s
Edge → core: plate crops 36 Mbps or keep crops at edge, pull on alert/search (→ ~0)
Edge → core: vehicle/person counts 1.9 Mbps 400 rows/s: one per analysed camera per 60 s
Control-room viewing 609 Mbps peak; 544 tiles, 10% at full resolution; district rooms sit beside their edge, so only the state room's wall (17.9 Mbps) crosses the backbone
SDC → DR: database replication 9.2 Mbps average WAL, 99 GB a day at 2× the stored bytes
Backhaul per district, peak 19.1 Mbps its share of metadata, crops and counts, plus the state room watching 16 of its cameras; links: 2 × 50 Mbps (5.2× headroom)

Low-bandwidth operation: the backbone carries metadata, not video. Browser preview uses a 640p H.264 profile (the camera's second stream where available, an on-demand transcode where not); full resolution is fetched only for the tile an operator opens. A view-only camera costs no bandwidth until someone watches it. When the backhaul drops, the edge keeps working: sightings spool to disk and replay in order when the link returns. In the meantime watchlist hits against the edge's cached copy of the watchlist are recorded at the edge (the worker's log, plate masked, and data/spool/<worker id>/local-alerts.jsonl) and reach the control room on replay, with the original sighting time (§7.1).

4.3 The alternative: carry and record every stream centrally (Model 4)

At the same per-camera bitrates used above:

Model 4 (central VMS) This design
Backbone, sustained 320 Gbps (80,000 × 4 Mbps) 2.7 Mbps metadata; 40.1 Mbps with crops and counts
Recording, 15 days 51.8 PB; 103.7 PB with a DR copy none: video stays on department NVRs; 16.0 TB of sightings, counts and crops

The 15 days is an ASSUMPTION: the upper end of what departments keep today. Continuous recording at a constant bitrate is also an assumption: motion-triggered or variable-bitrate recording would shrink the Model 4 storage figures, and even the sub-stream floor needs 10.4 PB.

Vehicle and person counts are one row per analysed camera (tiers A and B) per minute; the row size is measured on the PoC (512 bytes for the busiest camera, plus indexes). The window length is the lever: five-minute windows (--count-window-s 300) cut the table to 7 M rows and 4 GB a day, at the cost of coarser peaks.

Hot, warm and cold:

  • Hot (PostgreSQL, 90 days): sightings and counts are range-partitioned by time (monthly in the PoC, daily at statewide volume), so retention is DROP PARTITION, not a delete job. The PoC creates the partitions; the scheduled job that drops expired ones is design.
  • Warm (object store, 5 years): older partitions export to Parquet for yearly investigation queries (design).
  • Cold: evidence linked to an FIR is copied to a cold archive under legal hold; the audit log is exported daily under object lock (§7.2). Both design.
  • Video is never copied: it stays on department recorders under their own retention, and the registry records each camera's retention days so an investigator knows how long footage exists.

Retention periods are the Home Department's decision, not the software's; the values here are the model's ASSUMPTIONS.

6. AI Processing Capacity

6.1 Analytics tiers

Tier Share Cameras Analysis Continuous compute
A — ANPR 10% 8,000 5 fps, full decode yes
B — event analytics 20% 16,000 1 fps, keyframe-only decode yes (light)
C — view & record 70% 56,000 none; relayed only while watched no

Inference load: 56,000 frames/s statewide (tier A 40,000, tier B 16,000).

Tier shares are ASSUMPTIONS. The registry replaces them with fact as departments onboard. Tier B decodes keyframes only (skip_frame=nokey): at typical 1–2 s GOPs that gives 0.5–1 analysed frames/s at a fraction of the decode cost, and the model sizes tier B at the upper end, 1 fps.

This profile was built on 7 Oct as the counts-only worker (sentinel_analytics/counts.py; HLD 5.4) and measured on the development laptop: 40 simulator streams at once at 0.78 counted frames/s per stream (median) on 2.46 CPU cores and 13% of the GPU, with no plate worker running and the relay's CPU excluded; 50 streams saturate it at 0.34/s (bench/results/counts_capacity.md). The same evening it counted 7 of the organisers' sandbox cameras at 14–34 frames a minute each while the sandbox dropped every session about every 45 s (counts_sandbox.md). Keyframes 1–2 s apart cap the rate at 0.5–1 frame/s, as the model assumes; a camera whose keyframes are more than 4 s apart (three sandbox cameras keyframe every 10–15 s) is decoded in full so its zones do not miss short presences, and then costs tier A's decode, which the model does not price. Per-frame decode measured on the laptop with 14 simulator streams: 0.47 CPU cores keyframes-only against 2.57 decoding every frame (bench/results/keyframe_decode.md, 8 Oct; the 7 Oct probe gave 0.52 and 2.53). The 250 streams per node of §6.2 remains an ASSUMPTION: the laptop's CPU, not its GPU, ran out at 50 streams, NVDEC was not used, and a laptop figure is a direction. Unmeasured: the accuracy of the profile's counts (the same per-frame COCO detections, at about one frame a second), and the load of both profiles on one machine. Before 7 Oct a tier B camera ran the same worker as tier A, plate path included; a plate worker given a wide camera still does, and yields few plate reads, not none (the wide stock camera read 21.7% of the plates on screen for a second or more; HLD 7.5).

The GPU requirement is therefore 248 single-GPU nodes (§3.1), one GPU each. Per-node stream capacity is the most important assumption in this document and the first thing the PoC measures: run sentinel-bench --streams 8,16,32,64 on the candidate GPU-native node and replace --node-anpr-streams, and bench/counts_capacity.py for --node-event-streams. Watchlist matching, measured at 100,000 entries, keeps less than one database connection busy at the statewide average read rate. On one laptop, a database of 80,000 cameras and 3.0 million sightings took 706 sightings/s of ingest, each read matched against 100,100 watchlist entries, against the 556/s average (bench/results/core_scale.md, 24 Sep): a floor, not a sizing (§11).

Load balancing and horizontal scaling. Workers shard cameras across nodes by index (WORKER_INDEX / WORKER_COUNT, built), so capacity is added by adding nodes; a worker on another machine than the relay sets RELAY_HOST (built 7 Oct), since the registry stores relay addresses as localhost. The counts-only worker takes every analytics camera the plate workers do not (COUNTS_EXCLUDE), up to COUNTS_MAX_STREAMS. Today the shard is fixed when a worker starts; moving a failed node's cameras to its peers automatically is design (§7.1). The API is stateless and scales behind HAProxy (design); PostgreSQL adds read replicas for search and moves to daily partitions at statewide volume (design).

7.4 Security controls

Implemented in the PoC:

  • Role-based access (admin, supervisor, operator, department nodal officer, auditor) with department and district scoping enforced in queries, not in the UI
  • Append-only audit log of every login, plate search, movement query, onboarding and alert action; database rules make UPDATE and DELETE no-ops
  • Camera credentials never reach browsers — everything reads the relay; stream URLs are credential-masked in audit records and, since 8 Oct, in the registry listing and CSV export every reader sees; the onboarding form hides the pasted address while a screen is projected, and scripts/onboard_urls.py takes a list of addresses through the relay so the credentials stay in camera_sources
  • Internal ingest authenticated separately from user tokens, the key compared in constant time; a batch is capped at 5,000 sightings, and an event time without a timezone or far from now is refused
  • Sign-in: Argon2id verified off the event loop, more than 30 attempts a minute from one address refused before the password is checked (no lockout or MFA yet); an error logged at every start while a secret is still an example value, with scripts/rotate_secrets.py and scripts/set_passwords.py to replace them
  • Registry input validated on every write path (API, CSV import, catalogue sync): a camera code is letters, digits, - and _, a stream field is a stream address; the worker opens rtsp and http addresses only; enumerated query parameters answer 422 to a bad value
  • Audit also covers listings of a camera's recent sightings, watchlist checks of one plate and registry exports (8 Oct); a user scoped to a district alone is confined to it
  • The API's access log is off and the worker's sighting lines mask the plate (HLD 11.2)
  • Evidence crops served only with a valid token, the right permission and camera scope; there is no open static folder
  • PostgreSQL, Valkey, the media server's control API and metrics, and the simulator's control port bound to the host. Pushing a stream into the relay needs the publisher credential; an anonymous publish from the LAN is refused (checked 24 Sep), and a path already being published cannot be taken over (overridePublisher: no). Viewing a stream is still open on the local network: the relay authorisation hook below closes that
  • 23 negative security checks (scripts/security_checks.py) and 309 unit tests cover the above (HLD 11.2)
  • Face recognition deliberately absent (see privacy-dpdp.md)

Production additions (design):

  • Camera VLANs with no internet egress; edge nodes are the only bridge
  • mTLS between edge and core, TLS for every browser session; secrets in a vault (credentials_ref already modelled); encrypted disks on edge nodes, which hold crops and the spool outside the data centre; encrypted backups
  • Media relay authorisation delegated to the Sentinel API per path (MediaMTX HTTP auth hook), so RBAC governs who can watch which camera and nothing but the relay's own sources can publish
  • CERT-In incident reporting integration; STQC / third-party audit before go-live (priced in §9.2)

9.5 What is not bought

The largest saving is the spend this architecture avoids. A Model 4 central VMS would re-ingest and re-record all 80,000 streams centrally (§4.3):

Avoided by integrating instead of replacing Scale of the avoided cost
Central recording storage 51.8 PB of primary storage for 15 days (103.7 PB with its DR copy), duplicating what departments already record locally
Statewide ingest bandwidth 320 Gbps sustained to carry every main stream to a central site (64 Gbps even for sub-streams only), against ~2.7 Mbps of metadata in this design
Camera and recorder replacement Existing cameras, NVRs, DVRs and VMS licences are reused; no department is asked to re-procure
Per-camera VMS licensing No new per-channel licence: the platform reads one stream per camera over open protocols
Migration downtime Departments keep operating their own systems throughout; there is no cut-over

The model does not price the Model 4 alternative; the quantities above are what it would have to buy.

11. What the PoC must measure next

  1. Streams per GPU-native node for tier A (replaces the 60-stream assumption), and the node's power draw (replaces 0.5 kW)
  2. Keyframe-only decode density for tier B on the node (replaces 250). The profile is built; on the laptop it counted 40 streams at once on 2.46 CPU cores with the GPU at 13% (bench/results/counts_capacity.md), so the node's CPU, not its GPU, is the first thing to size. Its count accuracy against hand counts, and the load of both profiles on one node, are unmeasured too
  3. Real plate-read accuracy on government footage by camera class, to set anpr_viable. First figure from the organisers' sandbox: 90 of 120 (75.0%) stored plates exact, 107 (89.2%) within one character, almost all on the 2 of 30 views where plates are readable, checked blind against their crops by a team member on the control room's plate review page (bench/results/sandbox_review.md, 26 Sep). By Sentinel's confidence: 69 of 81 exact (85.2%) at 0.9 or above, 8 of 18 between 0.5 and 0.7, so high-confidence reads can pass and the rest go to a person; the page's bands set that floor and anpr_viable from real plates. An earlier check by our AI assistant, not blind, gave an optimistic 92 of 107. The plate-reading quality report (HLD 5.3) now tiers every camera from what it captures: on the sandbox, CAM06 and CAM07 are marginal (median plates 104 and 106 px across) and the other 28 counts-only, including the five the registry marks ANPR-viable (bench/results/capture_quality.md). Its grade describes the camera and does not predict a right read on real plates, so anpr_viable is set from the tier and blind reviews together. Still to measure: a second reviewer, the capture rate and the other camera classes
  4. Vehicles per ANPR camera per day on real junctions (replaces 6,000)
  5. Actual tier shares as departments onboard their fleets through the registry
  6. Core ingest, matching and search at peak rates on server hardware, with replication and several API instances (556 sightings/s and 400 count rows/s on average, higher at peak). One laptop sustained 706 sightings/s against the 556/s average on 24 Sep (bench/results/core_scale.md); count rows were not included
  7. Camera bitrates per district from the registry and the site survey (replaces the 4 and 0.8 Mbps assumptions in §4.1)
  8. Restore time from the first restore drill (replaces the RTO target in §7.1)
