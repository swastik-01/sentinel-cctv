1. Summary

Gujarat has roughly 80,000 government cameras spread across 26 departments, bought from different vendors, recorded on different NVRs and video-management systems, and kept for anywhere between 7 and 15 days. A vehicle seen on a police camera and again on a municipal camera five kilometres away is, today, two unrelated recordings.

Sentinel joins them up without replacing any of it. It is Model 2, unified viewing and selective analytics, built on Model 1, the registry and GIS foundation every entry must include:

  • Model 1 — Centralised registry and GIS is the base layer: every camera, whatever its make or owner, is a row with a location, an owner, a stream endpoint, a health state and a statement of what analytics its position allows.
  • Model 2 — Unified viewing and metadata analytics connects directly to department cameras and recorders over RTSP (ONVIF discovery planned), relays each stream once, and extracts vehicle movement metadata that is searchable statewide and matched in real time against watchlists fed by VAHAN and eGujCop (mock adapters that read local fixture files until live access is granted; their fields are modelled on the systems' published descriptions, not checked against the live interfaces).

The platform is working software, not a design exercise. Everything described as implemented below runs today and is exercised by a 34-check end-to-end test, 23 negative security checks, 189 edge-case checks, 111 control-room checks and 379 unit tests. Everything described as at scale is sized by a capacity model whose inputs are labelled as measured or assumed.

2. Design principles

  1. Integrate, don't replace. Departments keep their cameras, recorders, retention and ownership. Sentinel opens one upstream session per camera and never writes to department systems.
  2. Video stays local; metadata moves. Analytics runs next to the cameras. Only plate reads, events and alerts cross the state backbone — kilobits, not gigabits.
  3. Be honest about optics. Most CCTV is wide-angle, and plates are unreadable on it. The registry records which cameras are ANPR-viable, and analytics is tiered accordingly.
  4. Vendor-neutral and open. Open protocols (RTSP, WebRTC, HLS; ONVIF discovery planned) and open-source software, apart from the NVIDIA GPU driver and CUDA runtime (licence caveats in section 13). Vendor specifics live in adapters, never in the core.
  5. Accountable by construction. Every plate search, every movement query with its stated purpose, and every onboarding, watchlist and alert action is audited; face recognition is deliberately absent (sections 7.4 and 11.3).

3. Why Model 2, on the Model 1 foundation

Model Decision Reasoning
1 — Registry + GIS Built — the mandatory foundation The portal flags Model 1 as mandatory: the common registry and GIS layer that Models 2, 3 and 4 build on. Nothing else works without a trustworthy inventory — where each camera is, who owns it, whether it is up, what it can see.
2 — Unified viewing + selective analytics Built: the chosen model Delivers the live test case directly: onboarding, monitoring, plate tracking, watchlist alerts — without middleware and without touching department systems. The portal's own description of Model 2 covers every route and output we use: RTSP, ONVIF, vendor SDKs or APIs; ANPR metadata, event tagging, camera-wise indexing, searchable movement records, video walls and alerts, with no central storage of video.
3 — VMS federation Not needed Model 3 puts a federation middleware between the platform and each VMS. Sentinel connects directly instead. A VMS that will not re-stream over RTSP connects through its vendor's SDK or API, which Model 2 allows: one adapter per vendor, recorded in the registry (source_systems.adapter); designed, not built.
4 — Central VMS Not recommended now Re-ingesting and re-recording 80,000 streams centrally duplicates storage the departments already pay for (51.8 PB for 15 days), and needs 320 Gbps of sustained backbone at 4 Mbps per camera, 64 Gbps even for sub-streams only (capacity model, Plan for Scale §4.3).
5 — Hybrid / innovative Not claimed Model 1 is every entry's mandatory foundation, so Model 1 with Model 2 is Model 2. What Sentinel adds beyond Model 2's list (vehicle and person counts, intrusion zones, route reconstruction with speed checks, store-and-forward at the edge) is analytics and deployment on Model 2's relayed streams, not a second integration architecture; Model 4's central recording is deliberately left out.

On the submission form this is Model 2 – Unified Viewing and Selective Analytics, with Model 1 confirmed as the foundation.

3.1 Architecture principles, point by point

The problem statement requires an "open, modular, scalable, secure, standards-based, and vendor-neutral" architecture that avoids vendor lock-in and stays technology-agnostic. How each word is honoured:

Principle How Sentinel satisfies it
Open Every component is open source on published protocols, with two licence caveats: react-leaflet, the map bindings, is under the Hippocratic License, which is not OSI-approved, and both detectors are AGPL-3.0, which puts obligations on a deployment (section 13; a replacement under a permissive licence is planned, not yet trained). The NVIDIA GPU driver and CUDA runtime are proprietary but free of charge. No proprietary SDK is required to run it.
Modular Media relay, analytics workers, API, database and control room are separate processes with documented interfaces. Any one can be replaced without touching the others — the analytics worker, for instance, talks to the platform only through the /ingest endpoints (its camera list, the watchlist copy, sightings, events and heartbeats).
Scalable Workers shard cameras by index; the API is stateless behind a load balancer; sightings are time-partitioned. Capacity is added by adding nodes, not by redesign.
Secure RBAC with department scoping enforced in queries (reads and writes), append-only audit, credentials held server-side, evidence crops only behind a token, a permission and camera scope, the control plane bound to the host; per-path media authorisation at deployment.
Standards-based RTSP, WebRTC/WHEP, HLS, SQL/PostGIS, OpenAPI, JWT; ONVIF Profile S/T for discovery is planned (section 5.1). No bespoke wire formats.
Vendor-neutral No column, table or code path assumes a camera make or VMS. Vendor specifics live in adapters (source_systems.adapter, watchlist adapters). Checked with varied catalogue shapes in unit tests and with the simulator's RTSP streams (H.264 and H.265, published by FFmpeg); no vendor camera, NVR or VMS has been onboarded yet.
No lock-in Cameras, recorders and storage stay with the departments. The platform holds metadata, which exports to CSV; the API is described by OpenAPI (registry-api.json).
Technology-agnostic, future expansion New camera types arrive as URLs; new analytics attach to the same ingest contract; new government databases attach to the one adapter contract fetch() → watchlist entries.

4. Architecture

District edge (×33). A media relay (MediaMTX) holds exactly one upstream session per camera and fans it out: RTSP to the analytics nodes, WebRTC and HLS to viewers. GPU analytics nodes decode, detect, read and track, then publish metadata, in two profiles: every frame on the cameras that read plates, keyframes only on the rest (section 5.4). When the backhaul or the core is down, each analytics node writes the batches it cannot send to a bounded spool on disk and replays them oldest first, with their original frame times, once the core answers; meanwhile it matches plates against a cached copy of the active vehicle watchlist and records any hit locally (section 8.1; built and tested in the PoC). Plate crops stay at the edge and are fetched when an alert or a search needs them.

State core (GSDC, with DR). A stateless API tier, PostGIS for the registry and sightings, an event bus partitioned by district, the watchlist matcher, the append-only audit log, and adapters to government databases.

Control rooms. The state control room and district control rooms use the same web application: map, video wall, vehicle search, alerts, health and reports, registry, watchlist and audit trail.

5.2 Onboarding an unfamiliar feed

This is the flow for a feed handed over on the day. The operator pastes a URL; Sentinel probes it through the relay, learns the codec and resolution from the stream itself, and registers the camera with relay endpoints.

  • One upstream session. However many operators and analytics workers read a camera, the camera or recorder serves one RTSP session — to the relay.
  • Credentials stay server-side. Upstream URLs live in camera_sources; browsers and workers only ever see relay endpoints; audit records store the URL with the password masked. The onboarding form hides the pasted address while a screen is projected (show on demand), and a list of addresses goes in through the same path with scripts/onboard_urls.py, never through the registry's bulk CSV, which stores an address as given. A camera registered with a credentialed address anyway is shown to every reader with the credentials masked (GET /cameras, the CSV export; since 8 Oct).
  • On demand for view-only cameras. A camera without analytics connects upstream only while someone watches it, which is what makes tier C (70% of the estate) nearly free.
  • H.265. Browsers cannot be relied on to decode HEVC. The relay transcodes a 640p H.264 preview on demand; analytics always reads the original stream.
  • Self-healing. Relay configuration is re-asserted every health cycle, so a media-server restart repairs itself.

5.3 Health monitoring and gap analysis

Every 20 seconds the health monitor records reachability, latency and byte flow per camera (online, degraded when bytes stop arriving, offline) and publishes state changes live. An idle camera relayed on demand is checked by a TCP connection to its server, one per host and port each round, however many cameras sit behind it. The gap-analysis report flags districts with no cameras, districts with cameras but no ANPR-viable camera, and districts whose uptime falls below 70% (counting only cameras whose health is known), and ageing infrastructure: AMCs or warranties expired or ending within 90 days, cameras installed AGEING_YEARS (7) or more years ago, cameras under maintenance or faulty, and cameras with no installation year (decommissioned cameras are left out). Each camera's maintenance state, note, installation year and AMC expiry are registry columns, edited from the Registry page. Department reports show vendors, retention ranges and online counts per department.

Plate-reading quality by camera (built 26 Sep; Health & Reports page; /reports/camera-quality). Health says whether a camera streams; this says whether its plates can be read. Every stored plate capture is measured from its evidence crop and graded good, marginal or poor, with its reasons: size against published ANPR guidance (characters at least 10 px tall, 20–60 px best; plates 100–150 px across, at least 75 px), blur, darkness, low contrast, glare, two-line layout, and low reader confidence. The size cut-offs are the vendors' (Adaptive Recognition, Axis). The blur, light and contrast cut-offs come from a damage test on our own stock-footage crops: each crop was blurred, darkened, flattened or washed out step by step, and a cut-off is the point where fewer than 80% of our reader's reads survived (bench/results/quality_thresholds.md); no sandbox crop or blind review was used to set them. Captures are graded at ingest, and earlier sightings were backfilled (8,635 graded by 26 Sep 16:02). Each camera then gets:

  • a tier: A reads plates (at least 60% of captures good and a median plate at least 100 px across, from 20 or more captures), C counts and viewing only (under 20% good, median characters under 10 px, or no plate read), B marginal in between;
  • its captures, median plate width and character height, main issues, the blind-review exact rate where reviewed, and plates per 1,000 analysed frames;
  • recommendations of three kinds: camera (zoom in or move closer, faster shutter, infrared, WDR off, re-aim), model (retrain the reader with reviewed plates) and configuration (mark it ANPR-viable, or use it for counts and viewing only).

Captures a reviewer marked as not a plate (a hoarding, a sign) are left out. The report holds counts, medians and rates only, never plate text or a crop; it needs reports.read and is scoped to the user's department, like the other reports (a security check, section 11.2). Vehicle Search shows each sighting's grade beside its crop. On the sandbox (bench/results/capture_quality.md), CAM06 and CAM07 are tier B, with median plates of 104 and 106 px and 39% and 36% of captures under size; CAM12 is C on one capture; the other 27 are C with no plate read (CAM05's 24 captures were the hoarding). CAM12–CAM16, which the registry marks ANPR-viable, are flagged "marked ANPR-viable, but its captures do not bear that out". The report's tiers describe what a camera's captures support; the statewide tiers of section 12 are what each camera is assigned to run, and the report is how those assignments are checked against real captures. The grade describes the camera; on real plates it does not predict whether a given read is right (section 7.5).

What the sandbox replays. Each camera plays one fixed clip, recorded in June 2026 at one time of day, in a loop: its picture carries the recording's own date and time whatever our clock says (one camera always shows the same June evening), and identical readings of the same plates recurred between our runs, hours apart; the loop length could not be read from our runs (bench/results/sandbox_loop.md). So the time of a run says nothing about the light in a camera's picture: our 25 Sep evening run and 26 Sep morning run replayed the same footage at different points of each loop, and neither is a day or a night test (section 7.5).

Two profiles, since 7 Oct. The plate worker decodes every frame of its cameras: one laptop takes about four at about 4.5 analysed frames per second each with object detection on (24 Sep), or eight at 2.9 with it off (bench/results/decode.md). Reading plates needs that; counting does not. So a second worker, the counts-only worker (python -m sentinel_analytics.counts; the tier B profile of section 12, which the submission pack described as sized but not yet built), runs beside it as its own process and takes every other analytics camera at once. It asks FFmpeg (through PyAV) to decode keyframes only, counts at most one frame a second per camera, runs the vehicle and person detector in batches, watches the intrusion zones, and writes the same count rows as the plate worker, each marked with how its camera was sampled (section 7.6). A camera whose keyframes are more than 4 s apart is switched to decoding every frame on the open session, still counted at most once a second: otherwise it would be counted every 10–40 s and its zones would miss short presences (the sandbox's CAM07, CAM17 and CAM18 keyframe every 10–15 s, the others every 1–2 s; bench/results/counts_sandbox.md). COUNTS_EXCLUDE names the plate workers' cameras, so no zone alerts twice. Measured on 7 Oct on one laptop, no plate worker running, the relay's own CPU excluded: 40 simulator streams at 0.78 counted frames per second per stream (median) on 2.46 CPU cores and 13% of the GPU; 50 saturates it, at 0.34 (bench/results/counts_capacity.md, section 13). On 7 sandbox cameras it counted 14–34 frames a minute each over about 3 minutes in which the sandbox dropped every session at once at least twice (counts_sandbox.md). Two things are unmeasured, and said so: the combined load of a plate worker and the counts-only worker on one machine, and the accuracy of the counts (per-frame COCO detections at about one frame a second, with no auto-rickshaw class and misses at night, section 7.6). At the finale the plate worker reads the plate-readable cameras at full rate on one laptop and the counts-only worker counts the other cameras, 30–40 of them, on a second (section 12): about 50 government feeds are then onboarded, viewable and all analysed at once, not in rotation.

7. AI approach

7.1 ANPR pipeline

Detection. A YOLOv11n licence-plate detector runs on the GPU, batched across all cameras due in a cycle. It is unioned with a classical text-line finder (black-hat morphology), which localises dark characters on bright plates on footage the detector was never trained on. Every candidate must still survive OCR and Indian-plate validation, so the looser candidate set costs OCR time, not precision. FP16 is disabled automatically on GTX 16-series cards, which return NaN boxes in half precision.

Recognition. fast-plate-ocr's CCT-XS v2 global model (10 character slots, so 10-character Indian plates fit), batched across every crop in a cycle on the CPU. Confidence is the weakest character's probability, because one wrong character is what breaks a watchlist match. A sighting whose reads all agree (three or more) stores its confidence raised to between 0.9 and 0.99 for that agreement, so the stored confidence is a track score, not always the raw OCR probability. Since 8 Oct each sighting also stores how many reads stood behind it (reads) and how far the vote agreed (agreement, the weakest position's winning share; 1.0 for a single read), so an operator can tell a one-read 0.6 from a three-read 0.95 (section 7.5).

Indian plate normalisation. Reads are parsed against the standard layout (SS RR L{1–3} NNNN) and the BH series. Confusions are repaired by position: a state code is letters, an RTO code and the running number are digits, so O/0, I/1, B/8 are resolved correctly in each place. Invalid state codes are repaired by look-alike substitution (GI01… → GJ01…). Text that is not a plate — DVR timestamp overlays, camera IDs — is rejected, and so is text that fits the layout only after two or more of its digits are turned into letters (Not a plate, below).

Tracking and voting. A vehicle crossing a camera produces many reads. Reads within two edits of a live track join it. A new track is held until three reads have arrived or its confirmation window closes, then emits once. The window runs on the wall clock, not on frame time (when decoding falls behind, frame times lag and a frame-time window would close before a second read could arrive), and stretches with the camera's achieved analysis rate: at least one second and at least 2.5 analysis intervals. The stored plate is a per-character, confidence-weighted vote across the track's reads of the most common length, so a random single-frame error is outvoted; if the vote is not a valid Indian plate, the best single read is stored instead. The evidence crop is the most confident read.

A track seen only once is normally discarded as a likely mis-detection. On a camera analysed below 2 frames per second a vehicle may only ever be analysed once, so there a single read with OCR confidence of at least 0.6 is accepted, counted and logged; the API raises an alert on such a single-read sighting only when it matches a watchlist plate exactly. Unit tests reproduce the failure this fixes (no sightings at all at 0.8, 0.5 or 0.3 analysed frames per second, or with 1.5 s of frame lag) and pass.

After emission the same plate on the same camera is suppressed for 8 seconds, extended while it stays in view. A plate one character away from a plate the camera emitted within that window counts as the same vehicle; without that, one vehicle read two ways became two phantom vehicles. The trade-off: an early misread can become the stored plate for that pass.

Fixed text. On the organisers' sandbox a phone number on a hoarding, "75750 83008", passes the plate rules as TS75OB3008 and was stored 23 times in 25 minutes before this rule existed, and once in the hour and a half after it. A vehicle does not show the same text at the same spot of the picture again minutes later; fixed text does, and so does a parked vehicle. So a sighting whose text is similar to one stored at the same place (boxes overlapping) within 10 minutes is not stored, and similar text there stays muted until unread for 30 minutes (static_text.py, 7 unit tests built on the hoarding's own reads). "Similar" means a difflib ratio of 0.8, about one character in ten: the hoarding's two reads score 0.84, while two different plates of one RTO series pass that bar 0.2% of the time (at the first setting, 0.6, it was 30%, and one CAM06 plate was dropped on 25 Sep). Different vehicles stopping at one spot (a toll booth, a stop line) carry different plates and are all stored. The simulator loops each vehicle past the same spot every 60 s, so simulator runs turn the rule off (STATIC_TEXT_WINDOW_S=0).

Not a plate. The fixed-text rule still stores the first sighting of a piece of text: the hoarding was stored 24 times in all. Its phone number fits a plate layout only because the position-aware repair turns four of its digits into letters. So since 26 Sep a read is dropped when two or more of its letters had to be made from digits to fit a plate layout (state code, series and BH letters counted together); one such letter is a common misread and is kept. The rule runs on each read after the confidence and plate-layout checks and before the tracker (MAX_LETTERS_FROM_DIGITS, default 1; a large value turns it off), and the worker logs per camera how many reads it dropped, as counts only. Tiered analytics. At scale, cameras flagged ANPR-viable run the plate pipeline (tier A, 5 fps) and tier B cameras get event analytics only. A plate worker on a wide camera produces vehicle and person counts and reads only the few plates large enough (the wide stock camera read 21.7% of the plates on screen for a second or more, section 7.5), and the reads it makes are stored. Tier C cameras are relayed and recorded, not analysed.

7.2 Tracking across the network

Cross-camera tracking is plate identity plus geography plus time: the movement query returns every sighting of a registration number, including 1-edit OCR near-misses, ordered by frame time with camera locations. The search page shows it as a journey:

  • Visits. Consecutive reads at the same camera no more than five minutes apart are one visit (a stop), with its first time, how long the vehicle stayed and its best evidence crop. Stops are numbered on the map in order.
  • Hops. Between consecutive exactly-read visits at different cameras, the hop carries the distance between the two cameras, the travel time and the average speed. The distance is a straight line between cameras and the speed is an average over the leg, not a measured speed; the page says so.
  • Implausible speed. A hop of at least 1 km at more than 150 km/h is flagged: two vehicles carrying the same plate (a cloned plate) or a misread is more likely than the speed. Below 1 km, camera placement error dominates, so short hops are not flagged.
  • Near misses. A visit made only of one-character-off reads is kept, marked as a possible misread, and never used for a speed.

Every read stays available as a list and a CSV export. Where plates are unreadable, appearance-based vehicle re-identification is on the roadmap (section 14); it is deliberately not claimed as delivered.

7.4 Face recognition — a deliberate omission

The HLD requirement lists FRS among the analytics to describe, so we describe it and our position.

Sentinel does not perform facial recognition. Detecting that a person is present is not recognising who they are: Sentinel counts people (section 7.6) and notices a person inside an intrusion zone (section 7.7), and stores no box, image or track of any person. Wide-area biometric matching of people who are not suspects is hard to justify under the DPDP Act's purpose limitation and data minimisation, published accuracy varies across demographics, and a false biometric match in a policing context carries consequences a false plate read does not. Vehicle-based tracking answers the operational questions in the problem statement — stolen and blacklisted vehicles, BOLO, movement history — and wanted- and missing-person watchlist entries are operationalised through their associated vehicles.

If the department requires FRS, the pipeline accommodates it without redesign: it is another detector in the same batched inference step, writing to detection_events and matching against a gallery through the existing watchlist and alert path. What it would need first is a lawful basis, a retention and review policy, an accuracy and bias evaluation on Gujarat footage, and an audit regime at least as strict as the one already in place for plate searches. The ENABLE_FACE_RECOGNITION flag exists so the choice is explicit in configuration rather than buried in code. See docs/privacy-dpdp.md.

7.5 Accuracy — what we know and what we don't

Real Gujarat plates, checked blind by a person (the organisers' sandbox, our 25–26 Sep runs). On 26 Sep (12:28–13:02 IST) a team member checked 144 of the 145 real sightings Sentinel stored from the sandbox on the plate review page (section 7.8), signed in as the administrator account. The check is blind: the page shows only the evidence crop, the reviewer types the plate, and the server compares that reading with the stored read. 24 were a hoarding's phone number (a reading with no letters is graded "not a plate"). Of the 120 vehicle plates, 90 were exactly right (75.0%) and 107 within one character (89.2%); 13 were wrong, and none was marked unreadable (bench/results/sandbox_review.md). By camera: CAM06 71 of 92, CAM07 19 of 27, CAM12 0 of 1. The 25 Sep evening run (20:07–23:48 on our clock, all 30 cameras) gave 54 of 75 (72.0%), 68 within one character; the government-feed video and report cover that run, and were made before any review. The 26 Sep morning run (06:51–07:58, 8 cameras) gave 36 of 45 (80.0%) (bench/results/sandbox_daynight.md). Both runs replayed the same looping footage (section 5.4), so the split is by run, not by day and night.

The marking ships as a review aid, not as an accuracy change.

How far the check can be trusted. We tested the method where the true plates are known. On 226 blind reviews of our own cameras (synthetic plates, and plates painted onto stock footage) the reviewer was right 222 times (98.2%); of the 41 reviews the page graded one character off or wrong, the truth confirmed Sentinel's error in 37 and the reviewer's typing slip in 4. On the stock clip AMD-TRAF-002 the blind check found Sentinel right on 96.7%, matching the automated ground-truth figure of 96.8% (bench/results/review_truth.md, bench/review_truth.py). One person checked the sandbox plates; none has been checked by a second yet.

Why the 30 misses missed (bench/results/blind_errors.md). Our AI assistant looked at each missed crop with both readings in view and judged whose reading the crop supports: Sentinel's stored read was wrong on 13, the reviewer's reading on 13 (typing slips 5, a look-alike typed where the plate format fixes a letter or a digit 4, misreads 4; 10 of the 13 are not valid registrations as typed, 12 of all 30), both on 1, and on 3 the crop cannot say. Its own blind re-read of the crops, shuffled among hits with no reading shown, sided with the stored read on all 13 reviewer-side misses and against it on all 14 where Sentinel's read was wrong. That is an AI's judgement, not a person's, so it changes nothing: the quoted figure stays 90 of 120, and only a second blind reviewer can move it (section 14). Where Sentinel was wrong, the causes were a dropped character (a ten-character plate stored as nine) 5, a letter read as a similar letter 3, a digit misread 3, the vote choosing a short read 1, and the plate box stopping short of the plate's end 1 (the plate where both were wrong, a digit misread). Re-reading the stored crop alone repeats 13 of those 14 misreads, so they are the reader model's, not the vote's. Sentinel's own confidence is the strongest sign of a misread (AUC 0.84 on the misses where Sentinel was wrong, 0.72 on all 30); plate size, blur and brightness barely separate misses from hits (AUC 0.46–0.61).

Reading changes tried without training (bench/results/ocr_fix_trials.md). 25 changes to how a crop is read (test-time augmentation with three ways of combining the views, contrast enhancement, 9 other fast-plate-ocr models alone and in two ensembles, reading a two-line plate as two halves, upscaling) were fixed, with the rule for a gain, before any was scored on the 120 blind-checked crops. None met the rule on real plates. The one with the most net fixes, eight augmented views combined per character, read 93 of 120 exact against the shipped reader's 88 on the same single crops (6 fixed, 1 broken; not significant once corrected for 25 trials), broke two-line plates on our own feed, and cost about ten times the CPU. Every other available model read fewer plates exactly (the next best 53 of 120): the shipped reader is the best of those available. The Indian-format fine-tune ft1 was then trained and scored on the frozen sets (bench/results/ocr_ft1.md, 26 Sep): on the 120 blind-checked real plates it read 89 exact against the shipped reader's 88 on the same crops (8 fixed, 7 broken; p = 1.0), and 66 against 69 among the 81 stored at confidence 0.9 or above. It passed its gates as non-inferior, showed no gain on real plates, and is not adopted: the shipped reader runs at the finale. (The stored sightings, voted over several frames, were 90 of 120; the single-crop re-read is 88.)

Does the capture grade predict a right read? Not on real plates. On the 120 blind-checked sandbox plates, captures graded good (section 5.3) were exact 53 of 66 (80.3%), marginal 29 of 44 (65.9%) and poor 8 of 10 (80.0%), with overlapping intervals; the one reason whose captures were read clearly worse was the reader's own low confidence (1 of 6 exact) (bench/results/capture_quality.md). On our stock-footage clips, with thousands of sightings, the grades do order the error rate (good 99.1%, marginal 97.5%, poor 92.6% exact against the painted plates), but those are the clips the blur and light cut-offs were set on, so that is not an independent test, and a plate too small or blurred to read leaves no sighting to grade. So the grade describes a camera's captures and drives the advice in section 5.3; it does not say whether a given read is right. Sentinel's confidence does that better (above).

An earlier, optimistic figure. Before the review page existed, our AI coding assistant compared each stored read with its crop, with the stored read in view, so not blind and not a person: 92 of 107 (86.0%) exact, after leaving 14 crops out as unreadable (bench/results/sandbox_eye.md; 55 of 66 in the evening run, 37 of 41 in the morning run). On the same 8 cameras the two runs stored 4.6 (evening) and 4.1 (morning) plates per 1,000 analysed frames, and the toll-plaza camera (CAM12) stored none in the morning run's 1,925 frames: two samples of the same looping footage, so the difference is where each camera's loop was, not the light (bench/results/sandbox_daynight.md). 7.6 Vehicle and person detection — how it works and what we measured

Counts. One row per camera per minute, holding the denominator and the numerators together: frames analysed, detections per class, and the most seen in any single frame. A detection is a box in one frame, not a unique vehicle: nothing is tracked, so the platform reports in view (average per analysed frame) and peak, which do not change with the sampling rate, rather than a count of vehicles that would mostly count one parked car many times. A camera analysed with nothing in view reports zero against its frame count; a camera not analysed reports nothing. Since 7 Oct the counts-only worker writes the same rows from keyframes, about one frame a second per camera, and every row says how its camera was sampled (detail.profile: keyframes, or every-frame for a camera whose keyframes are far apart, section 5.4); in view and peak do not depend on the sampling rate, so a row from one frame a second is a coarser sample of the same thing. On the simulator's synthetic cameras the counts are honestly zero (the vehicles are drawn rectangles); on the stock traffic footage of AMD-TRAF-001 (real vehicles) they read about 13 vehicles and 2 people in view, peaking at 21 and 7, and 79–88% of the two stock-footage cameras' sightings carry a vehicle type (two 10-minute windows, 23 and 24 Sep). People include riders and passengers: COCO boxes a scooter and its rider separately.

Type and colour. A plate belongs to the smallest vehicle box that contains it and whose centre it sits near. When two vehicles of different classes overlap the plate side by side, or one vehicle is boxed as two classes, the answer is NULL. Auto-rickshaws have no COCO class, so their sightings stay NULL too. Type is a confidence-weighted vote across the vehicle's reads. Colour comes from flat body panels (glass, plate, tyres and box edges masked), needs strong saturation before naming a colour, and reports white and silver as one class: a CCTV frame has no exposure reference to separate them. It abstains on IR or greyscale frames and on two-wheelers, where the pixels would be the rider.

7.7 Intrusion zones — how it works and what we checked

Drawing a zone. A user who may edit the camera draws a polygon on its live video on the video wall and sets which classes trigger it (person by default, or any of the detector's six classes), a minimum dwell (3 s by default), armed hours in IST (an overnight span such as 22:00–06:00 works; no hours means always armed) and a severity. Zones are kept in the camera's registry record in fractions of the frame, so they survive a resolution change, and every change is audited. The workers read them when they start, so a change takes effect at the next restart. The counts-only worker watches the zones of its cameras at about one frame a second (a camera whose keyframes are far apart is decoded in full so a short presence is not missed, section 5.4); COUNTS_EXCLUDE keeps each camera on one worker, so no zone alerts twice.

Detection. The worker tests the boxes the object detector already produces (section 7.6), so a zone adds no inference. A box is inside when its ground point, the bottom centre where a person stands or a vehicle's wheels are, lies inside the polygon. Presence tolerates missed frames: an occupancy ends only after the zone has looked empty for at least 2.5 analysis intervals. One occupancy that lasts the minimum dwell raises one event, carrying the zone, the classes and the most seen at once, the dwell and the time. The API turns it into an alert of type intrusion with the zone's severity, at most one per zone per camera per 120 s, through the same path as watchlist alerts (section 8.1). Zones are drawn over the wall tiles, and a tile flashes when its zone fires. With object detection off, configured zones are not watched and the worker says so.

Privacy. Presence in the zone is the trigger; identity is never used. No box, crop or track of the person is stored or sent: the event is the zone, classes, counts, dwell and time, and the operator opens the live view to see who it is.

What was checked (24 Sep 2026). One demo zone, "kerb strip (simulated restricted area)", on AMD-TRAF-001 (stock footage, a 60-second loop). In 15 minutes it raised 14 events, one per loop, for the same scene each time (one or two people counted inside the zone; two in 10 of the 14). With a 60 s loop and a 120 s cooldown the next alert came two or three loops later, so the 14 events became 6 alerts. An offline replay of one loop (bench/zone_check.py) gave one event; its saved frame shows a pedestrian inside the zone; motorcycle riders on the carriageway beside it did not trigger it. One clip and one zone show the mechanism end to end. They are not a false-alarm rate, which needs hours of footage per camera with the zone's real geometry, by day and at night.

Crops are served by sighting id, because a crop's file name carries the plate. Every review is audited as plate.review with the sighting, mode and verdict, and a change to one as plate.review.edit with the first and the new verdict, never the plate text. Blind checks are best done from auditor accounts, because a supervisor can look a read up in Vehicle Search first. Three security checks cover the page (section 11.2), two edge-case and three control-room checks the change to a saved reading, and two edge-case and one control-room check the plate-by-plate list.

Twelve of the readings behind the 30 sandbox misses are not valid registrations as typed (section 7.5). The page still records a reading exactly as typed and does not warn: a warning would nudge an unsure reviewer into guessing a valid plate. A second reader settles such slips instead (section 14).

On 24 Sep, adding a stock-footage plate found 48 earlier sightings and raised 1 alert.
Link outage Built and tested. A hit is recorded at the edge only, in the worker's log (the plate masked, section 11.2) and in data/spool/<worker id>/local-alerts.jsonl, with the API's one-edit rule and 120 s cooldown (it does not apply the single-read rule, so the edge can record a hit the control room will not raise). Nothing reaches the control room during the outage. When the API answers again the spool replays oldest first with the original frame times, a batch id makes a replay after a lost reply store once, and the control-room alert is raised on replay with the original sighting time. Intrusion events spool the same way and alert on replay. Each worker process has its own spool folder (data/spool/<worker id>, with its watchlist copy and local-alerts file; 8 Oct, 2 unit tests): two workers on one machine replaying one folder took each other's files mid-read. A worker that starts while the API is unreachable waits for it (up to 5 minutes by default, STARTUP_WAIT_S, retrying every 2 s and backing off to 30 s) instead of exiting.

Outage test, 24 Sep 00:01–00:03 IST. The API was stopped for ~87 s while the worker ran on one camera (AMD-AMC-001, simulator). 21 sightings were spooled, and all 21 replayed within 0.5 s of the first attempt after the API returned; the worker's retry backoff had reached 30 s, so replay began about 20 s after the API was back. 20 local alerts were recorded at the edge (the worker's log and its local-alerts file). The control-room alerts carried the original sighting times and were raised 79–84 s after them, which is the outage.

10. Data model and retention

  • detection_events holds the counts: one row per camera per minute, with frames analysed in value and detections and peaks per class in detail. Numerator and denominator cannot be separated, and the row has no box, label or track; since 7 Oct detail.profile says how the camera was sampled, keyframes or every-frame (section 5.4). Intrusion events land in the same table (event_type intrusion): zone, classes, the most seen at once, dwell and time, and no box or track either.
  • vehicle_sightings.reads and agreement (8 Oct) hold how many reads stood behind the stored plate and the vote's weakest position's winning share: a count and a share, no new personal data. The "check the crop" mark (section 7.5) is computed from the stored confidence when a sighting is read, not stored.
  • Intrusion zones live in the camera's registry record (cameras.tags), as polygons in fractions of the frame; no schema change was needed.
  • alerts holds watchlist hits and intrusion alerts (alert_type), a retrospective flag for hits raised when a plate was added, and a detail record (the zone, or the measured latency and whether the sighting was replayed from an outage spool).
  • watchlist_masks holds one-character masks of every watchlist plate for near-miss matching, kept by a trigger. ingest_batches holds the ids of stored ingest batches for at least 14 days (older ids are purged when the API starts), so a replayed batch is stored once.
  • plate_reviews holds each person's verdict on a stored read (section 7.8): mode, what they typed, the verdict, and a copy of the read and its confidence, so results outlive the sighting's retention. The typed readings are real registrations: they stay in the database, and only totals are published.
  • vehicle_sightings and detection_events are range-partitioned by time; retention is enforced by dropping partitions, not by delete jobs. Monthly partitions in the PoC, daily at statewide volume.
  • Plate text is indexed both exactly and by trigram, so near-miss search stays fast at scale.
  • camera_sources holds upstream URLs server-side; production moves secrets to a vault and keeps only credentials_ref.
  • audit_log has database rules that turn UPDATE and DELETE into no-ops.

11.3 Privacy under the DPDP Act

  • No face recognition. Proportionality, uneven accuracy across demographics, and the treatment of biometric data make statewide face matching of non-suspects hard to justify. Vehicle-based tracking answers the operational questions in the problem statement.
  • Minimisation. The platform stores metadata and small plate crops, not video. Footage stays on department recorders under their existing retention. Vehicle and person counts are rows of numbers per camera per minute, with no box, image or track; the end-to-end test checks this. Vehicle type and colour are written onto the plate sighting they describe and inherit its retention and audit.
  • Intrusion zones trigger on presence, not identity. The event and the alert hold the zone, the classes and how many were inside, the dwell and the time; no box, image or track of the person is stored or sent. The operator looks at the live view.
  • The edge copy of the watchlist is minimal. It holds plate, kind and severity only, with no names, reasons or case references.
  • Purpose. Movement queries accept a stated purpose, recorded in the audit trail.
  • Traceability. Every watchlist entry carries a source and a reference; stale entries expire.
  • Oversight without access. The auditor role reads the audit trail and reports, including the aggregate counts, but cannot watch footage or search plates. It can check stored plate crops on the plate review page (section 7.8), and each review is audited without the plate text.

12. Scaling to 80,000 cameras

The companion Plan for Scale derives every number from bench/capacity.py. In brief:

  • Tiering — 10% ANPR (8,000 cameras at 5 fps), 20% event analytics (16,000 at 1 fps on keyframes; the profile is built, the counts-only worker of section 5.4, and measured on one laptop), 70% view-and-record with no continuous compute. Shares are assumptions to be replaced by registry data.
  • Edge compute — about 248 GPU nodes (≈7.5 per district) at an assumed 60 ANPR streams, or 250 keyframe-only event streams, per node plus 25% headroom. Streams per node is the most important ASSUMPTION in the model and the first thing the pilot measures. On the laptop the keyframe profile counted 40 streams at once on 2.46 CPU cores with the GPU at 13%, no plate worker running (bench/results/counts_capacity.md): its CPU ran out first, NVDEC was not used, and a laptop figure is a direction, not a node figure, so 250 stays an assumption.
  • Two machines (the finale) — the first deployment on more than one host: laptop A runs the database, the API, the relay and the control room, and the plate worker on the plate-readable cameras; laptop B runs the counts-only worker on every other camera (RELAY_HOST points it at A's relay; CORS_ORIGIN_REGEX and the relay's WEBRTC_HOST let a browser on B open A's control room, written by scripts/lan.ps1) and is the hot spare: scripts/db_backup.ps1 dumps the database and copies the crops to a USB stick every 10 minutes, scripts/db_restore.ps1 restores them on B and starts its API, and B's relay rebuilds every camera path from camera_sources within about one health interval (20 s). Up to 10 minutes of sightings are lost in a hand-over; the statewide answer is the standby below. The combined load of both workers on one machine is unmeasured.
  • Why GPU-native nodes — the 17 Sep benchmark showed the laptop's GPU at 21–33% utilisation while the CPU sat at 100%: software decode was the ceiling. Hardware decode is now built and on by default; on the laptop, where the decoder is the Intel integrated GPU's, it raised the analysed rate per stream by 49% at 8 and at 12 streams, and made no difference at 4 (section 13). That shows decode offload relieves the CPU; it does not stand in for NVDEC on a server node, which is unmeasured. The decoded frames still pass through CPU memory for preprocessing; keeping them on the GPU from decode to inference is the production design. A laptop measurement is a direction, not a node figure, so 60 streams per node stays an assumption.
  • Backbone — ~2.7 Mbps of metadata on average statewide; video does not cross it for analytics.
  • Storage — ~2.6 TB of sightings for 90 days; ~11.5 TB of plate crops for 30 days, at the edge.
  • HA/DR — node N+1, store-and-forward spools (built and tested), self-healing relays (built and tested), stateless API, synchronous database standby and asynchronous DR replica (design).
  • Watchlist matching — ~1 ms per plate read at 100,000 entries (measured), so matching the statewide average of 556 reads a second keeps less than one database connection busy.
  • The core at statewide size — run on one laptop (bench/results/core_scale.md, 24 Sep): a database of 80,000 cameras, 3.0 million sightings over 30 days and 100,100 watchlist entries took 706 sightings/s of ingest, each read matched against the watchlist (request median 516 ms, p95 718 ms), against the model's statewide average of 556/s. A 30-day movement history with near misses took 337 ms (median) and a registry page 46 ms. One API process and the database on one laptop: a floor, not a sizing. Above 2,000 cameras the map shows counts per grid cell and, zoomed in, loads only the cameras in view (built): 161 ms for the state view against 6 s and 45 MB for the whole registry as one layer.
  • Cost-benefit — ≈₹39.7 crore indicative capex and a five-year total cost of ownership of ₹100.7 crore (unit prices are ASSUMPTIONS), against the central-VMS spend it avoids: 51.8 PB of duplicated recording for 15 days and 320 Gbps of statewide ingest at 4 Mbps per camera (64 Gbps even for sub-streams only), with the operational benefits and how to measure them.
  • Department-wise information requirements and a four-wave statewide rollout plan — Plan for Scale §10 and §8.

Known limitations, stated plainly

  • Accuracy on real Gujarat plates rests on 120 plates from the organisers' sandbox, checked blind against their crops by one team member: 90 (75.0%) exact, 107 (89.2%) within one character; no second reviewer has checked them yet (sections 7.5, 7.8). Our AI assistant's review of the 30 misses puts 13 of them on the reviewer's reading, not Sentinel's; only a second blind reviewer can settle that, and the quoted figure stays 90 of 120 until one does. Plates were readable on 2 of the 30 views, and the capture rate is unmeasured. The sandbox replays fixed looping clips, so our two runs are not a day and night comparison. The other figures in section 7.5 are synthetic plates painted onto stock footage.
  • The plate-reading quality grade (section 5.3) describes a camera's captures. On the 120 blind-checked real plates it does not predict a right read (section 7.5), and it cannot see plates that were never read.
  • No reading change tried without training beat the shipped reader on real plates, and the Indian-format fine-tune ft1, trained and scored on the frozen sets, was non-inferior with no gain on real plates and is not adopted (section 7.5): the shipped reader runs at the finale.
  • The two stock-footage cameras are Pexels clips filmed on a Mumbai road, whose signs are visible in the frame; their Ahmedabad names and map positions are simulated, like every simulator camera.
  • The vehicle/person detector is stock COCO: it has no auto-rickshaw class, often confuses bus and truck (hence the vote), labels vans and pickups as trucks, and misses people in dense crowds and at night. An Indian-road model (e.g. trained on IDD) is the production path. Colour is a heuristic.
  • Intrusion zones were checked on one clip and one zone (section 7.7); there is no false-alarm rate yet. A zone change takes effect when the worker restarts.
  • Simulated feeds loop the same vehicles through every camera, so simulated movement histories are not physically plausible journeys; on real feeds the timeline follows the vehicle.
  • One laptop reads plates on about four streams at full rate (eight with object detection off), and a second counts every other camera at once from keyframes (section 5.4). The two workers on one machine, and the accuracy of the keyframe profile's counts (the same per-frame COCO detections, at about one frame a second), are unmeasured.
  • During a backhaul outage a watchlist hit is recorded only at the edge (the worker's log and a file under its spool folder); no screen shows it until the link returns and the control-room alert is raised on replay.
  • Government database adapters are mocks over local fixture files, with fields modelled on the systems' published descriptions and never checked against the live interfaces; live access needs authorisation.
  • The core was run at statewide size on one laptop only (section 13): peak rates, several API instances, database replication and the event bus are not load-tested; watchlist matching was measured at 100,000 entries on one connection (Plan for Scale §6.2 and §11).
  • The worker's hardware decode was measured on the laptop's Intel integrated GPU, not on NVDEC; NVDEC was measured for decode alone on a laptop GTX 1650 (nvdec_decode.md); decode on a server GPU node is unmeasured.
  • Both detectors run on Ultralytics YOLO and are licensed AGPL-3.0: the fine-tuned plate detector (YOLOv11n) and the stock COCO vehicle/person model (YOLO11n). They are open source, but a deployment must either publish Sentinel under a compatible licence (straightforward for a government-owned platform), obtain a commercial licence, or retrain both under a permissive framework, which for the COCO model means a general-purpose detector as well as the plate fine-tune. A licence-clean replacement is planned for the pilot, not the finale: YOLOX detectors (Apache-2.0) run by ONNX Runtime (MIT), with the plate model retrained only on images whose licences allow commercial use. Nothing has been trained. It replaces the current models only if it passes adoption gates we wrote down before training (bench/detector_gates.md): no loss of plate capture or exact reads on our own feeds, no loss of plate yield on the sandbox cameras, and no slower analysis. Until then the AGPL models are what runs, at the finale too, and every number in this document was measured with them. We never quote the plate model's published scores: its own model card says they are inflated by an overlap between its training and test images. Every accuracy figure here is our own measurement. The plan covers the two detectors only: the OCR model (MIT code) stays, and its author does not document what its weights were trained on.
  • The not-a-plate rule (section 7.1) rests on one hoarding's text on one sandbox camera, chosen after seeing it; it does not stop signage made of letters. On the two sandbox cameras that read plates it dropped no read in 135 minutes, the hoarding in view (bench/results/plate_gate_live.md). A learned classifier was not built and stays on the roadmap.
  • react-leaflet, the map bindings in the control room, is under the Hippocratic License 2.1, which is not an OSI-approved open-source licence: it requires use consistent with the UN human-rights principles and settles disputes by public arbitration. A police deployment has it cleared by legal review, or replaces it with direct calls to Leaflet (BSD-2). PyAV, which the counts-only worker decodes through, is BSD-3, but the wheel it installs bundles a GPL build of FFmpeg, used for decoding only; a deployment builds PyAV against its own LGPL FFmpeg or accepts the GPL terms, as it already does for the FFmpeg in the relay image. The other components are MIT, BSD, Apache-2.0, PostgreSQL, LGPL/GPL (FFmpeg) or GPL-2.0 (PostGIS) licensed.
  • The PoC runs a single PostgreSQL instance. Viewing on the media relay is open to anyone on the local network who knows a stream path (the relay authorisation hook is roadmap). Its control API and metrics are bound to 127.0.0.1. Pushing a stream in needs the publisher credential, an anonymous publish from the LAN is refused (checked 24 Sep), and a path already being published cannot be taken over (overridePublisher: no); the credential is one shared secret in the PoC, and per-source credentials come with the relay auth hook.
  • Map tiles come from the OpenStreetMap public tile server, whose usage policy does not permit production load; deployment self-hosts tiles.
