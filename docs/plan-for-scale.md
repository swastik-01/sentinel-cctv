# Plan for Scale — 80,000 cameras

Sentinel's statewide design rests on three decisions, each forced by a measurement or a
property of the camera estate rather than by preference:

1. **Not every camera needs continuous analytics.** Cameras are tiered by what their
   position allows, and the registry records it (`anpr_viable`, `analytics_enabled`).
2. **Video stays where it is.** Departments keep recording on their own NVR/VMS.
   Analytics runs at district edge nodes; only metadata crosses the backbone.
3. **Frames never touch CPU memory at scale.** Our own benchmark shows the CPU, not the
   GPU, is what runs out first — so production nodes decode and infer on the GPU.

Every sizing and cost number below is generated by [`bench/capacity.py`](../bench/capacity.py)
(`python bench/capacity.py`; `--help` lists every input). Each input is labelled **GIVEN** (the
problem statement or the PoC's own configuration), **MEASURED** (on the PoC, dated) or
**ASSUMPTION**, and all of them are listed in Appendix A. Change an
assumption and re-run. Unit prices are placeholders to be replaced by GeM quotes. Anything marked
**design** is specified here but not built in the PoC.

## 1. What we measured

`sentinel-bench` on the development laptop: GTX 1650 Ti (4 GB), Intel i7-10750H with
6 logical CPUs, 16 GB RAM, Windows 11 + Docker Desktop. Full pipeline per stream:
software H.264/H.265 decode → YOLOv11n plate detector (CUDA, FP32) + text-line finder →
batched OCR (fast-plate-ocr CCT-XS v2, CPU). Target: 5 analysed frames/s per stream.

| Streams | Decoded fps (total) | Analysed fps / stream | Batch latency | GPU util | GPU memory | Host CPU |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (1080p25) | 28 | **4.8** | 91 ms | 26% | 171 MB | 96% |
| 2 (1080p25) | 56 | **4.8** | 130 ms | 33% | 171 MB | 98% |
| 4 (mixed) | 89 | 3.5 | 222 ms | 26% | 253 MB | 100% |
| 8 (mixed) | 160 | 1.3 | 452 ms | 21% | 493 MB | 100% |

Source: `bench/results/bench_20260917_174834.json`.

**What this tells us**

- **The GPU is idle; the CPU is saturated.** GPU utilisation never exceeds a third.
  Software decode of every frame (inter-frame codecs cannot skip reference frames),
  colour conversion and image preprocessing consume the host.
- **Confounder, stated plainly:** an antivirus service was using ~41% of the machine at
  idle during these runs. The absolute numbers are pessimistic; the *shape* — CPU-bound,
  GPU-idle — is the finding.
- Optimisations already in the worker: grab-every-frame but convert only ~1.5× the analysis
  rate; batched OCR with capped threads; on-demand browser transcodes (measured 17 Sep: idle
  media-server CPU from ~130% to ~23% of a core); FP16 auto-disabled on GTX 16xx cards, which
  return NaN boxes in half precision.
- Hardware decode, measured afterwards on the same laptop (24 Sep, `bench/results/decode.md`, two
  rounds, plate pipeline without object detection): with D3D11 decode through OpenCV
  (`HW_DECODE=auto`, now the worker's default) analysed fps per stream rose from 1.93 to 2.88 at
  8 streams and from 0.91 to 1.34 at 12 (+49% both), and system CPU at 8 streams fell from 94% to
  80%; at 4 streams nothing changed. On this laptop the D3D11 decoder is the Intel UHD integrated
  GPU's, not NVDEC (Windows GPU-engine counters, 24 Sep; the NVIDIA decoder stayed at 0%). It shows
  that decode offload relieves the CPU. NVDEC on the node is still unmeasured; on another laptop's GTX 1650, decode only (9 Oct, `bench/results/nvdec_decode.md`), the NVIDIA decoder took about 740 frames a second of 1080p H.264 at its ceiling (about 29 streams at 25 fps) and about 1,500 of 1080p H.265 with the resize on the GPU, and at 16 real-time 1080p streams cut system CPU from 89% to 31% (H.264, decoded and resized to 640 px) and from 75% to 32% (H.265). In today's
  worker frames still go to the CPU after decode, and a laptop result does not replace the per-node assumption in §6.2.

**Consequence for production:** GPU-native nodes. Hardware decode (NVDEC via GStreamer
`nvcodec` or FFmpeg `cuvid`) keeps frames in GPU memory; preprocessing and inference run
on the same device (ONNX Runtime CUDA/TensorRT execution provider); only boxes and crops
return to the CPU. The worker's interfaces (`RtspSource`, `PlateDetector`, `PlateOCR`)
are the seams where that swap happens without touching the rest of the platform.

## 3. Hardware & Software Requirements

### 3.1 Edge node

The node is a single-GPU server because that is what the capacity model sizes; §6 derives how many.

| Network | 2 × 10 GbE (camera side, uplink) + 1 GbE management | ingest at capacity: 240 Mbps (tier A node), 200 Mbps (tier B node) |
| Load at capacity | tier A: 60 streams, 300 frames/s; tier B: 250 streams, 250 frames/s | ASSUMPTION until measured on the node |
| Power | ~0.5 kW average | ASSUMPTION; feeds the power line in opex |
| Price | ₹15.0 lakh | ASSUMPTION; GeM quote needed |

Store-and-forward: a fully loaded node spools ~181 MB a day of sightings and counts; the PoC default
cap (`SPOOL_MAX_MB=512`) covers 3.0 days, 1.3 GB covers 7. Production sets `SPOOL_MAX_MB` for the
7-day target. Since 8 Oct each worker process spools under its own folder (`data/spool/<worker id>`,
with its watchlist copy and local-alerts file), so a plate worker and a counts-only worker on one node
never replay each other's files; the cap is per worker.

Candidate nodes are benchmarked in the pilot with `sentinel-bench`. If another accelerator, or a
two-GPU node, wins on cost per stream, the node count and the node price change together. In the
PoC, decoded frames are handed to the CPU for preprocessing (§1); keeping them on the GPU from
decode to inference is design.

The cost model carries **no software licence fees** (§9.3). Two licences carry obligations:

- **Ultralytics YOLO (AGPL-3.0).** A government deployment publishes its changes under a compatible
  licence, or buys a commercial Ultralytics licence (not priced here), or retrains both detectors
  under a permissive framework. Both detectors run on Ultralytics YOLO, licensed AGPL-3.0. A
  licence-clean replacement is planned for the pilot, not the finale: YOLOX detectors (Apache-2.0) run
  by ONNX Runtime (MIT), with the plate model retrained only on images whose licences allow commercial
  use. Nothing has been trained. It replaces the current models only if it passes adoption gates we
  wrote down before training (`bench/detector_gates.md`): no loss of plate capture or exact reads on
  our own feeds, no loss of plate yield on the sandbox cameras, and no slower analysis. Until then the
  AGPL models are what runs, at the finale too, and every number in this pack was measured with them. We never quote
  the plate model's published scores: its own model card says they are inflated by an overlap
  between its training and test images. Every accuracy figure here is our own measurement. The plan
  covers the detectors only; the OCR model's training data is not documented by its author. Grafana
  and Loki are also AGPL-3.0, but they run unmodified as separate services, so their licence does
  not reach Sentinel's code.
- **react-leaflet (Hippocratic License 2.1)**, the map bindings in the PoC's front end. It is not an
  OSI-approved open-source licence: it requires use consistent with the UN human-rights principles and
  settles disputes by public arbitration. A police deployment has it cleared by legal review, or
  replaces it with direct calls to Leaflet (BSD-2).
- **PyAV (BSD-3) with a bundled GPL FFmpeg.** The counts-only worker (7 Oct) decodes through the PyAV
  wheel, whose FFmpeg is a GPL build, used for decoding only. A deployment builds PyAV against its own
  LGPL FFmpeg, or accepts the GPL terms as it already does for the FFmpeg in the relay image; on the
  edge node the GPU decode of row 2 replaces it in any case.

## 4. Network & Bandwidth Planning

### 4.1 Camera → edge

| Leg | Cameras | Per camera | Average district | Statewide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tier A: main stream, continuous | 8,000 | 4 Mbps | 970 Mbps | 32.0 Gbps |
| Tier B: sub-stream, continuous | 16,000 | 0.8 Mbps | 388 Mbps | 12.8 Gbps |
| Tier C: only while watched | 56,000 | 0 when idle | ≤ 17.9 Mbps (one 16-tile wall) | ≤ 609 Mbps (every wall) |
| **Continuous total** | 24,000 |  | **1.36 Gbps** | **44.8 Gbps** |

Video stays inside the district network for analytics, but this leg is not small: the analysed
cameras put about 1.36 Gbps into an average district's edge. Averages hide the skew, since cameras
concentrate in the cities, so each district's network is sized from its registry count during
onboarding. Three consequences:

- The edge rack needs 10 GbE aggregation from the camera VLANs, priced as district network works
  in §9.2 and confirmed by the site survey.
- A camera behind a thin link (a remote checkpost on a cellular link, say) cannot be analysed at the
  district edge at 4 Mbps. For those sites the design option is a small edge box beside the camera
  or NVR, not priced here.
- Tier B pulls the camera's sub-stream. The object detector already resizes every frame to 640 px
  (`object_imgsz`), so a 640p stream gives it about the same pixels at a fifth of the bitrate.
  Tier A needs the main stream: the plate is read from the full-resolution crop.

### 4.2 Edge → core and viewing

| Flow | Rate | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Edge → core: metadata | 2.7 Mbps | average; 556 sightings/s |
| Edge → core: plate crops | 36 Mbps | or keep crops at edge, pull on alert/search (→ ~0) |
| Edge → core: vehicle/person counts | 1.9 Mbps | 400 rows/s: one per analysed camera per 60 s |
| Control-room viewing | 609 Mbps | peak; 544 tiles, 10% at full resolution; district rooms sit beside their edge, so only the state room's wall (17.9 Mbps) crosses the backbone |
| SDC → DR: database replication | 9.2 Mbps | average WAL, 99 GB a day at 2× the stored bytes |
| Backhaul per district, peak | 19.1 Mbps | its share of metadata, crops and counts, plus the state room watching 16 of its cameras; links: 2 × 50 Mbps (5.2× headroom) |

Low-bandwidth operation: the backbone carries metadata, not video. Browser preview uses a
640p H.264 profile (the camera's second stream where available, an on-demand transcode
where not); full resolution is fetched only for the tile an operator opens. A view-only
camera costs no bandwidth until someone watches it. When the backhaul drops, the edge keeps
working: sightings spool to disk and replay in order when the link returns. In the meantime
watchlist hits against the edge's cached copy of the watchlist are recorded at the edge (the worker's
log, plate masked, and `data/spool/<worker id>/local-alerts.jsonl`) and reach the control room on replay,
with the original sighting time (§7.1).

### 4.3 The alternative: carry and record every stream centrally (Model 4)

At the same per-camera bitrates used above:

|  | Model 4 (central VMS) | This design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backbone, sustained | 320 Gbps (80,000 × 4 Mbps) | 2.7 Mbps metadata; 40.1 Mbps with crops and counts |
| Recording, 15 days | 51.8 PB; 103.7 PB with a DR copy | none: video stays on department NVRs; 16.0 TB of sightings, counts and crops |
| Floor: record only the 0.8 Mbps sub-stream | 64 Gbps; 10.4 PB (20.7 PB with DR) |  |

The 15 days is an ASSUMPTION: the upper end of what departments keep today. Continuous recording at
a constant bitrate is also an assumption: motion-triggered or variable-bitrate recording would shrink
the Model 4 storage figures, and even the sub-stream floor needs 10.4 PB.

## 5. Storage & Retention Strategy

| Data | Per day | Retention | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Video | — | stays on department NVR/VMS (7–15+ days) | not duplicated |
| Sightings (Postgres, partitioned) | 48 M rows / 29 GB | 90 d hot | 2.6 TB |
| Plate crops (object store at edge) | 384 GB | 30 d | 11.5 TB |
| Vehicle/person counts (Postgres, partitioned) | 35 M rows / 21 GB | 90 d hot | 1.9 TB |
| Warm archive (Parquet on the core object store) | — | 5 years | ≤ 90 TB (upper bound: database bytes, before compression) |
| Backups (base backups + WAL, at DR) | 99 GB WAL | 2 weekly fulls, 14 d point-in-time | 10.3 TB |
| Edge spool (store-and-forward) | ~181 MB per node | until replayed | 1.3 GB per node for 7 d |
| Alert evidence | small | per case / legal hold | cold archive |
| Audit log | small | legal retention | WORM export (object lock) |

Vehicle and person counts are one row per analysed camera (tiers A and B) per minute; the row
size is measured on the PoC (512 bytes for the busiest camera, plus indexes). The window length is
the lever: five-minute windows (`--count-window-s 300`) cut the table to 7 M rows and 4 GB a day, at the cost of coarser
peaks.

Hot, warm and cold:

- **Hot** (PostgreSQL, 90 days): sightings and counts are range-partitioned by time (monthly in
  the PoC, daily at statewide volume), so retention is `DROP PARTITION`, not a delete job. The PoC
  creates the partitions; the scheduled job that drops expired ones is design.
- **Warm** (object store, 5 years): older partitions export to Parquet for yearly investigation
  queries (design).
- **Cold**: evidence linked to an FIR is copied to a cold archive under legal hold; the audit log is
  exported daily under object lock (§7.2). Both design.
- **Video** is never copied: it stays on department recorders under their own retention, and the
  registry records each camera's retention days so an investigator knows how long footage exists.

Retention periods are the Home Department's decision, not the software's; the values here are the
model's ASSUMPTIONS.

## 6. AI Processing Capacity

### 6.1 Analytics tiers

| Tier | Share | Cameras | Analysis | Continuous compute |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A — ANPR | 10% | 8,000 | 5 fps, full decode | yes |
| B — event analytics | 20% | 16,000 | 1 fps, keyframe-only decode | yes (light) |
| C — view & record | 70% | 56,000 | none; relayed only while watched | no |

Inference load: **56,000 frames/s** statewide (tier A 40,000, tier B 16,000).

Tier shares are **ASSUMPTIONS**. The registry replaces them with fact as departments
onboard. Tier B decodes keyframes only (`skip_frame=nokey`): at typical 1–2 s GOPs that gives
0.5–1 analysed frames/s at a fraction of the decode cost, and the model sizes tier B at the upper
end, 1 fps.

This profile was built on 7 Oct as the counts-only worker (`sentinel_analytics/counts.py`; HLD 5.4) and
measured on the development laptop: 40 simulator streams at once at 0.78 counted frames/s per stream
(median) on 2.46 CPU cores and 13% of the GPU, with no plate worker running and the relay's CPU excluded;
50 streams saturate it at 0.34/s (`bench/results/counts_capacity.md`). The same evening it counted 7 of
the organisers' sandbox cameras at 14–34 frames a minute each while the sandbox dropped every session
about every 45 s (`counts_sandbox.md`). Keyframes 1–2 s apart cap the rate at 0.5–1 frame/s, as the model
assumes; a camera whose keyframes are more than 4 s apart (three sandbox cameras keyframe every 10–15 s)
is decoded in full so its zones do not miss short presences, and then costs tier A's decode, which the
model does not price. Per-frame decode measured on the laptop with 14 simulator streams: 0.47 CPU cores
keyframes-only against 2.57 decoding every frame (`bench/results/keyframe_decode.md`, 8 Oct; the 7 Oct probe
gave 0.52 and 2.53). The 250 streams per
node of §6.2 remains an ASSUMPTION: the laptop's CPU, not its GPU, ran out at 50 streams, NVDEC was not
used, and a laptop figure is a direction. Unmeasured: the accuracy of the profile's counts (the same
per-frame COCO detections, at about one frame a second), and the load of both profiles on one machine.
Before 7 Oct a tier B camera ran the same worker as tier A, plate path included; a plate worker given a
wide camera still does, and yields few plate reads, not none (the wide stock camera read 21.7% of the
plates on screen for a second or more; HLD 7.5).

### 6.2 Edge compute and GPUs

| Item | Value | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tier A nodes | 134 | 8,000 / 60 streams per node (ASSUMPTION) |
| Tier B nodes | 64 | 16,000 / 250 streams per node (ASSUMPTION; the laptop counted 40 at once, §6.1) |
| Headroom | 25% | N+1 failover and growth |
| **Edge GPU nodes** | **248** | ≈ 7.5 per district, co-located with district control rooms |
| GPUs at the core | 0 | the core matches, searches and stores; it runs no inference |
| Watchlist matching at the core | 0.63 busy connections | 556 reads/s × 1.14 ms median (MEASURED at 100,000 entries) |

The GPU requirement is therefore 248 single-GPU nodes (§3.1), one GPU each. Per-node stream
capacity is the most important assumption in this document and the first thing the PoC measures:
run `sentinel-bench --streams 8,16,32,64` on the candidate GPU-native node and replace
`--node-anpr-streams`, and `bench/counts_capacity.py` for `--node-event-streams`. Watchlist matching, measured at 100,000 entries, keeps less than one
database connection busy at the statewide average read rate. On one laptop, a database of 80,000
cameras and 3.0 million sightings took 706 sightings/s of ingest, each read matched against 100,100
watchlist entries, against the 556/s average (`bench/results/core_scale.md`, 24 Sep): a floor, not a
sizing (§11).

**Load balancing and horizontal scaling.** Workers shard cameras across nodes by index
(`WORKER_INDEX` / `WORKER_COUNT`, built), so capacity is added by adding nodes; a worker on another
machine than the relay sets `RELAY_HOST` (built 7 Oct), since the registry stores relay addresses as
`localhost`. The counts-only worker takes every analytics camera the plate workers do not
(`COUNTS_EXCLUDE`), up to `COUNTS_MAX_STREAMS`. Today the shard is fixed when a worker starts; moving a
failed node's cameras to its peers automatically is design (§7.1). The API is stateless and scales behind HAProxy (design); PostgreSQL adds read replicas for
search and moves to daily partitions at statewide volume (design).

## 7. Disaster Recovery Strategy

### 7.1 High availability and failover

| Component | Failure | Behaviour | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| District backhaul or API | link or API down | Workers spool batches to disk, keep matching plates against a cached copy of the active vehicle watchlist and record local alerts; on reconnect they replay oldest first with the original frame times, and the control-room alert is raised on replay | **Tested** 24 Sep: ~87 s API outage, 21 sightings spooled and all replayed, alerts raised with the original sighting times |
| Media relay | restart | API re-registers every relay within one health interval | **Tested**: 2/2 relays restored after a forced MediaMTX restart |
| Camera / NVR | offline | Health monitor flags it within one interval (20 s); uptime and gap reports record it | Built |
| Camera stream | keeps dropping the session | The worker reconnects with backoff from 2 s to 30 s, and starts again from 2 s only after 60 s of healthy streaming, so a source that drops every session a few seconds in is not retried every 2–5 s (on 25–26 Sep the sandbox dropped our sessions three times and twice refused our login) | Built 26 Sep; 2 unit tests |
| Edge analytics node | node down | Its cameras move to peers within the 25% headroom | Design: the shard is fixed at worker start today, so peers take the cameras when restarted with a new `WORKER_COUNT` |
| A worker on another host | the relay and the workers on different machines | `RELAY_HOST` points a worker at the relay machine; `CORS_ORIGIN_REGEX` at the API and `WEBRTC_HOST` at the relay let a control room on another machine sign in and play video (`scripts/lan.ps1` writes both and restarts the relay and API) | Built 7 Oct; the finale runs the plate worker on one laptop and the counts-only worker on a second |
| The single core host (PoC) | the machine running the database, API and relay fails | Hot spare by hand: `scripts/db_backup.ps1` dumps the database and copies the crops to a USB stick every 10 minutes; `scripts/db_restore.ps1` restores them on the spare and starts its API, whose relay rebuilds every camera path from `camera_sources` within about one health interval (20 s); workers are repointed with `API_URL` and `RELAY_HOST`. Up to 10 minutes of sightings are lost; the standby below is the statewide answer | Built 8 Oct (scripts); the drill is in the finale runbook |
| API | instance down | Stateless behind HAProxy | Design (the PoC runs one instance) |
| PostGIS primary | host down | Synchronous standby promoted (Patroni); no committed data lost within the SDC | Design (the PoC runs one instance) |
| State Data Centre | site loss | Asynchronous replica at the DR site promoted; RPO minutes, RTO under 4 h | Design; RPO and RTO are targets, measured by the restore drill below |

### 7.2 Backup and restore

| Item | How | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Continuous WAL archiving, point-in-time recovery | pgBackRest ships WAL from the primary to the DR repository as it is written; any moment in the last 14 days can be restored | Design |
| Base backups | Weekly full to the DR repository, encrypted; two fulls kept, 10.3 TB with 14 days of WAL (§5) | Design |
| Warm archive | Parquet on the SDC object store, three replicas. There is no copy at DR in this bill of materials, so a site loss would lose sightings and counts older than the 90-day hot tier until one is added (≤ 90 TB after five years, §5) | Design; DR copy not priced |
| Audit log, WORM copy | Nightly export of the day's `audit_log` rows to the object store under object lock (compliance mode), each day's file hashed together with the previous day's hash, so any later edit is detectable | Design. Built: database rules make UPDATE and DELETE on `audit_log` no-ops |
| Restore drill | Quarterly: restore the latest base backup and WAL to a scratch host, run the 34-check end-to-end smoke test against it, and record the time taken as the measured RTO | Design |
| Edge nodes | Stateless apart from crops and the spool; rebuilt from a golden image. Crops tied to an alert or case are copied to the core | Design |
| Registry and relay configuration | Held in the database; relays are rebuilt from it | Built: relays re-register from the registry after a media-server restart |

### 7.4 Security controls

Implemented in the PoC:
- Role-based access (admin, supervisor, operator, department nodal officer, auditor) with
  department and district scoping enforced in queries, not in the UI
- Append-only audit log of every login, plate search, movement query, onboarding and alert
  action; database rules make UPDATE and DELETE no-ops
- Camera credentials never reach browsers — everything reads the relay; stream URLs are
  credential-masked in audit records and, since 8 Oct, in the registry listing and CSV export every
  reader sees; the onboarding form hides the pasted address while a screen is projected, and
  `scripts/onboard_urls.py` takes a list of addresses through the relay so the credentials stay in
  `camera_sources`
- Internal ingest authenticated separately from user tokens, the key compared in constant time; a
  batch is capped at 5,000 sightings, and an event time without a timezone or far from now is refused
- Sign-in: Argon2id verified off the event loop, more than 30 attempts a minute from one address
  refused before the password is checked (no lockout or MFA yet); an error logged at every start while
  a secret is still an example value, with `scripts/rotate_secrets.py` and `scripts/set_passwords.py`
  to replace them
- Registry input validated on every write path (API, CSV import, catalogue sync): a camera code is
  letters, digits, `-` and `_`, a stream field is a stream address; the worker opens rtsp and http
  addresses only; enumerated query parameters answer 422 to a bad value
- Audit also covers listings of a camera's recent sightings, watchlist checks of one plate and
  registry exports (8 Oct); a user scoped to a district alone is confined to it
- The API's access log is off and the worker's sighting lines mask the plate (HLD 11.2)
- Evidence crops served only with a valid token, the right permission and camera scope; there is
  no open static folder
- PostgreSQL, Valkey, the media server's control API and metrics, and the simulator's control port
  bound to the host. Pushing a stream into the relay needs the publisher credential; an anonymous
  publish from the LAN is refused (checked 24 Sep), and a path already being published cannot be
  taken over (`overridePublisher: no`). Viewing a stream is still open on the local network: the relay
  authorisation hook below closes that
- 23 negative security checks (`scripts/security_checks.py`) and 309 unit tests cover the above (HLD 11.2)
- Face recognition deliberately absent (see [privacy-dpdp.md](privacy-dpdp.md))

Production additions (design):
- Camera VLANs with no internet egress; edge nodes are the only bridge
- mTLS between edge and core, TLS for every browser session; secrets in a vault (`credentials_ref`
  already modelled); encrypted disks on edge nodes, which hold crops and the spool outside the data
  centre; encrypted backups
- Media relay authorisation delegated to the Sentinel API per path (MediaMTX HTTP auth hook),
  so RBAC governs who can watch which camera and nothing but the relay's own sources can publish
- CERT-In incident reporting integration; STQC / third-party audit before go-live (priced in §9.2)

### 9.5 What is *not* bought

The largest saving is the spend this architecture avoids. A Model 4 central VMS would re-ingest and
re-record all 80,000 streams centrally (§4.3):

| Avoided by integrating instead of replacing | Scale of the avoided cost |
|---|---|
| Central recording storage | 51.8 PB of primary storage for 15 days (103.7 PB with its DR copy), duplicating what departments already record locally |
| Statewide ingest bandwidth | 320 Gbps sustained to carry every main stream to a central site (64 Gbps even for sub-streams only), against ~2.7 Mbps of metadata in this design |
| Camera and recorder replacement | Existing cameras, NVRs, DVRs and VMS licences are reused; no department is asked to re-procure |
| Per-camera VMS licensing | No new per-channel licence: the platform reads one stream per camera over open protocols |
| Migration downtime | Departments keep operating their own systems throughout; there is no cut-over |

The model does not price the Model 4 alternative; the quantities above are what it would have to buy.

## 10. Department-wise information requirements

Onboarding is only as fast as the information each department supplies. This is the complete ask.
Its per-camera rows (inventory, stream access, make, model and codec, retention and storage, which
cameras can read plates, ownership) are columns of the CSV template in the platform (Registry →
Template); the department-level rows (nodal officer, credentials policy, network path, recorder or
VMS details) and the installation and firmware details are collected on a department sheet.

| What we need | Why | Blocking? |
|---|---|---|
| Nodal officer: name, phone, email | One accountable contact per department for access and incidents | Yes |
| Camera inventory: code, name, latitude, longitude, address | The registry and the map; a camera without a location cannot be searched geographically | Yes |
| Stream access: RTSP/ONVIF URL per camera (or NVR/VMS host, channel list, API endpoint) | The relay needs exactly one way in per camera | Yes |
| Credentials, and the policy for rotating them | Held server-side in the secret store; never in a browser | Yes |
| Network path: VLAN/subnet, firewall rule needed from the district edge | Analytics runs beside the cameras, not over the internet | Yes |
| Camera make, model, codec, resolution, frame rate | Tiering and capacity planning; read from the stream where not supplied | Preferred |
| Installation year, AMC or warranty expiry, firmware version | Ageing-infrastructure gap analysis and replacement planning; matching vendor security advisories | Preferred |
| Recorder type and retention days, storage location (local/cloud) | Retention reporting and evidence retrieval | Preferred |
| Which cameras face traffic at a readable angle | Sets `anpr_viable`, and therefore which tier the camera lands in | Preferred |
| Ownership: government or privately shared, and the sharing agreement | Access scoping and the audit trail | Preferred |
| Existing VMS and version, and whether RTSP re-streaming is permitted | Decides direct RTSP integration versus a vendor SDK adapter | If a VMS is involved |

Anything in the "preferred" rows that is missing is inferred from the stream or left blank and
reported in the gap analysis, so onboarding is never blocked on paperwork that can follow.

## 11. What the PoC must measure next

1. Streams per GPU-native node for tier A (replaces the 60-stream assumption), and the node's power
   draw (replaces 0.5 kW)
2. Keyframe-only decode density for tier B on the node (replaces 250). The profile is built; on the
   laptop it counted 40 streams at once on 2.46 CPU cores with the GPU at 13%
   (`bench/results/counts_capacity.md`), so the node's CPU, not its GPU, is the first thing to size. Its
   count accuracy against hand counts, and the load of both profiles on one node, are unmeasured too
3. Real plate-read accuracy on government footage by camera class, to set `anpr_viable`. First
   figure from the organisers' sandbox: 90 of 120 (75.0%) stored plates exact, 107 (89.2%) within one
   character, almost all on the 2 of 30 views where plates are readable, checked blind against their crops by a
   team member on the control room's plate review page (`bench/results/sandbox_review.md`, 26 Sep).
   By Sentinel's confidence: 69 of 81 exact (85.2%) at 0.9 or above, 8 of 18 between 0.5 and 0.7, so
   high-confidence reads can pass and the rest go to a person; the page's bands set that floor and
   `anpr_viable` from real plates. An earlier check by our AI assistant, not blind, gave an optimistic
   92 of 107. The plate-reading quality report (HLD 5.3) now tiers every camera from what it captures: on the
   sandbox, CAM06 and CAM07 are marginal (median plates 104 and 106 px across) and the other 28 counts-only,
   including the five the registry marks ANPR-viable (`bench/results/capture_quality.md`). Its grade describes
   the camera and does not predict a right read on real plates, so `anpr_viable` is set from the tier and blind
   reviews together. Still to measure: a second reviewer, the capture rate and the other camera classes
4. Vehicles per ANPR camera per day on real junctions (replaces 6,000)
5. Actual tier shares as departments onboard their fleets through the registry
6. Core ingest, matching and search at peak rates on server hardware, with replication and several
   API instances (556 sightings/s and 400 count rows/s on average, higher at peak). One laptop
   sustained 706 sightings/s against the 556/s average on 24 Sep (`bench/results/core_scale.md`);
   count rows were not included
7. Camera bitrates per district from the registry and the site survey (replaces the 4 and 0.8 Mbps
   assumptions in §4.1)
8. Restore time from the first restore drill (replaces the RTO target in §7.1)
